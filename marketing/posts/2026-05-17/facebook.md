The tightness in your chest at 11pm — the one you push aside because you're too tired to deal with it — tends to still be there at 6am.

Osho, the philosopher and spiritual teacher, made a point that illuminates why: it isn't the emotion that causes the most trouble. It's the resistance to it. When we push away what we feel, the feeling doesn't disappear — it tends to show up later, somewhere in the body. In tense shoulders. In a jaw that won't unclench. In a night that doesn't quite rest.

Cathier was built around a different first step.

Before asking "how do you feel today?" the app asks something more specific: what do you notice in your chest right now? Is your throat relaxed or tight? Are your shoulders carrying something you haven't named yet?

Then comes the emotion naming — not just "stressed" or "sad," but one of 79 specific words. The difference between "forlorn" and "bereft" might seem small. But they describe different shapes of loss, with different sources and different next steps.

The goal isn't to analyze yourself or fix anything quickly. It's simply to see clearly what's there. That first act of noticing — without immediately trying to dismiss or resolve — is often where something begins to shift.
