# Fear isn't vague. It has a specific address in your body.

Fear isn't vague. It has a specific address in your body.

Throat closing. Belly going cold. Feet losing their ground.

Your nervous system is doing its job — delivering a precise physical report: *not safe here.* But most of us compress this whole signal into "I'm anxious" or "just stressed" and try to push through.

The problem with compression: you can't respond to something you haven't located.

Cathier's awareness dictionary holds 8 distinct words for fear. Not to complicate things — to locate them:

凛然 (lǐnrán) — the chill along your spine when an external threat arrives. You feel it in your back, your neck, the nape.
怯然 (qièrán) — the held breath of knowing you're not ready, going forward anyway. It lives in the chest — tight, small.
悚然 (sǒngrán) — the sudden full-body contraction when danger appears without warning. Everywhere, just for a second.

Each one asks for something different.

凛然 wants you to assess — what's actually threatening here?
怯然 wants permission — it's okay not to be ready.
悚然 wants grounding — feel your feet, your weight, the floor.

When you can name which kind of fear — and locate it in your body — you shift from reaction to witness. Not avoiding it. Not performing calm. Just: I see you. Here. In this spot.

That gap between sensation and recognition — that's where the practice lives.

Cathier's body scan starts with the body, not the label. First locate. Then name. Then choose how to meet it.

What does fear feel like in your body right now?
